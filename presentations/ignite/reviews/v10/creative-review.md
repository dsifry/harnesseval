# Creative review for revision 10

Reviewed all 20 native Keynote PNGs individually, the overview, and all written/spoken copy in `work/deck/story.json`. This is an adversarial creative assessment, not an imitation of a named advertiser. It combines art direction, graphic design, and advertising copy judgment. Technical conclusions still require the science reviewer's source checks. No new photography is recommended.

## Verdict

The baseline has an honest voice, good contrast, and a strong idea. It is not yet an exceptional five-minute performance. The main weakness is rhythm: the audience moves through a succession of large numbers, three nearly identical charts, a belated methodology slide, and four nearly identical typographic exhortations. There is too much administrative context near the opening and not enough tangible evidence near the action. Changing the headline font or adding a beautiful photograph will not repair that.

The exceptional version should feel like Dave stopped a room full of confident advice and put one claim on the test bench. Every visual should either make that test comprehensible, expose a surprise, or teach the audience what to do tomorrow. The drama is the evidence itself.

## Ranked changes

1. **Move campaign breadth to slide 3; replace slide 15's late scope recap with an actual FAIL/PASS specimen.** Fifty PRs and five codebases earn confidence at the opening. At minute 3:30 they interrupt the move from limitations to action and repeat 2,416. A verified original/fixed test result makes 'evidence' concrete and bridges the 59 misses to the reproducible development loop. Use actual retained output with exact provenance. This must not imply that the harness automatically reproduces or fixes findings.
2. **Make the selected comparison a three-frame visual sequence, not three separate chart explanations.** Register the configuration identities and row positions precisely across slides 11–13. Use horizontal zero-based bars, direct values, and a stable right-hand value column. The audience should only have to notice what changed. Scope this as one selected pair in the speech and visibly keep both configurations identified.
3. **Unclutter the harness drawing.** The lower lane is visually pinched between long labels, merge brackets, and a nearly touching Combine/Findings arrow. The large headline consumes too much vertical space. Make the parallel reviewers the visual event. Reduce the headline to 'Give review a process.' or keep the existing headline at a smaller size, and leave space around the merge. Do not illustrate unmeasured verification or automatic fixes.
4. **Make slide 10's uncertainty legible before making it dramatic.** 'No clear winner' is better oral language than 'statistically resolved,' but the picture must not imply equivalence. Retain the 17/4/1 split, use three horizontal bars, and label the largest 'No clear difference.' A compact subtitle must preserve 'high vs. medium' and benchmark score. The audience should never infer that high was proven useless.
5. **Turn the 59 misses into visible absence.** Fill the 88 found dots ivory. Draw the 59 missed dots as clearly visible hollow orange rings, not another filled orange block. Directly label found/missed. Orange is the item under discussion, not a universal good/bad code. Do not let the audience confuse the new orange defects on slide 5 with the missed defects on slide 14.
6. **Cut explanatory filler from the script and let difficult numbers land.** 'This was something we could measure' adds nothing after a measured result. 'At the median,' 'original benchmark score,' and selected-pair qualifications matter, but the remaining copy can be plainer and slower. Do not fill every 15-second block with 35 words.
7. **Treat the final three slides as an intentional closing campaign.** Their repetition can be earned if the preceding late scope poster becomes concrete evidence. Keep one strong action per slide and strong type. A small native fork-and-merge motif on slide 18 can recall the harness. Do not add numbered takeaway badges, company names, source clutter, fake terminal chrome, or decorative photographs.

## Visual language

- Keep the near-black stage field, warm ivory, and orange. It fits a dim room and is recognizably this talk.
- Helvetica Neue throughout. One regular and one bold weight are sufficient. No gratuitous monospaced 'computer' type.
- Orange means attention. Every chart still gets direct labels, and missing data is distinguished by fill state or explicit labeling. Color alone never defines the claim.
- Preserve the same dot coordinates across 4, 5, and 14. That turns the 147-bug set into a remembered object.
- Squares are comparisons; circles are defects. Remove the 72-square matrix on slide 3 to avoid introducing a second, subtly different square grammar before it is explained.
- Prefer a small number of enormous, useful marks over dense nominal precision. Source qualifications belong in notes and speech unless needed to prevent a visible misunderstanding.
- Use the rotary-control photograph once. It is a useful visual question and a welcome change of texture. More moody hardware images would make this an aesthetic theme rather than an argument.
- Keep visible content comfortably inset. Thin arrows, faint gray axes, and outline dots should survive projector black levels; do not make them hairlines.

## Bold alternate direction considered

An editorial 'evidence board' could replace nearly every poster with giant annotated specimens, matched comparison cards, a real test failure, and a recurring orange proof mark. This would be distinctive, but literal clipboard/laboratory/forensic styling would be costume. Avoid it. Adopt its discipline instead: a finding, the comparison that supports it, the caveat that changes its meaning, then the action. The slides should feel designed from the study's evidence, not from a presentation template.

## Optional spoken draft for the most useful changes

These are proposed lines, to be reconciled against the source review and actual timed delivery. No claim of an aloud rehearsal has been made.

1. 'Everybody seemed to have AI figured out. My friends had better prompts. Vendors had better tools. And somehow, all that confidence was making me less confident. What was I missing?'

2. 'Buy the course. Comment “AI agents.” Get the perfect system. I was comparing myself to everybody else's claims. So I changed the question: what actually works, when we compare it fairly?'

3. 'I ran 2,416 AI code reviews. Eight models. Three review approaches. Three reasoning levels. The full campaign covered fifty pull requests across five codebases. But first, I needed an answer key.'

4. 'For the deep comparison, I used six difficult pull requests from two codebases. The benchmark listed forty-two bugs. Now I could check what a review caught. Except that answer key was incomplete.'

5. 'The reviews surfaced another hundred and five verified defects. Each had a test that failed on the original code and passed with its fix. That brought the total to 147.'

7. 'In thirty-nine of forty-two matched comparisons, the harness found more verified bugs. Same model. Same reasoning setting. The review process made a difference.'

10. 'High reasoning was not a dependable shortcut. Against medium, seventeen of twenty-two comparisons had no statistically clear difference on the original benchmark score. Four favored high. One favored medium.'

11. 'Here is one comparison. Fable, high reasoning, one prompt: fifty-one bugs. Open-weight GLM Flash, low reasoning, in a harness: seventy-one. Twenty more bugs. This compares two complete setups.'

14. 'Even the highest-recall setup missed fifty-nine of the 147 bugs. Fifty-nine. These tools found real defects. But a clean review is never a guarantee that your code is correct.'

15. '[Use source-validated defect example here.] The test failed on the original code and passed with the fix. That is what makes a finding useful: I can check it.'

18. 'Use a harness. Give reviewers focused jobs and a repeatable process. Then check the findings. In this study, changing the process usually found more bugs than asking the same model once.'

20. 'The next time someone promises the perfect AI workflow, ask what they tested. Ask me, too. You do not have to trust the vibes. Follow the evidence.'

## Do not do

- Do not add a worried-developer stock image, a cinematic lab, a fake incident, or a second metaphor photograph just to make the deck feel 'visual.'
- Do not decorate the talk with brand logos, terminal borders, browser chrome, small captions, arbitrary geometric flourishes, or progress indicators.
- Do not replace honest qualifiers with punchier overclaims. 'Start low' is a recommendation. 'Low wins' is a different claim.
- Do not display all report caveats on the live slides. Do preserve the distinctions whose omission would make the visible headline false.
- Do not call this exceptional because all slides are clean. The new version earns its improvement by making the evidence easier to follow and the actions easier to remember.
