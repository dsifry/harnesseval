Science beats vibes: what actually finds bugs in AI code review

Everyone has an opinion about which model to use, whether an agent harness is worth it, and how much “thinking” to pay for. We measured it: eight models × three ways of working × three effort levels, scored against 147 verified bugs on the six highest-severity pull requests, inside a campaign of 2,416 review runs across a 50-PR public benchmark. Here is what you can use this week.

By Dave Sifry (GitHub · LinkedIn). This is the short version for working developers. Researchers and sceptics: every number here comes from the full report, which has the method, the intervals, the caveats and a reproduction chain.
Disclosure. I wrote metareview (MRV), one of the harnesses tested here. It is open source (MIT) and free. Every harness was judged by the same frozen instruments, and the data and code to re-run the analysis are public. Full disclosure and safeguards →

Finding · harness

A harness finds more real bugs than a single prompt

“One-shot” means pasting the diff into a single, carefully engineered prompt (read the exact prompt → REPORT §2.2). A harness makes the same model work the review in passes, with tools and specialised reviewers. We tested the open-source harnesses Compound Engineering (CE) and metareview (MRV). Holding the model and effort level fixed, the harness found more verified bugs in 39 of 42 comparisons, a mean gain of 13.5 points of recall.

What it costs you. A harness spent a median of 10× the tokens, about 5× the dollars and 3.5× the wall-clock time of the same model run one-shot. At low effort the median harness review took 2.8 minutes, against 54 seconds for one-shot. It also says more: the median harness configuration produced 5.5 unsupported findings (“hallucinations”) across the six reviews against 0 for one-shot, alongside 27.5 accepted useful suggestions against 6.5. More signal, but more to triage.

Finding · cost

The expensive part is the model, not the harness

Because a harness multiplies tokens, it multiplies whatever your model charges. Run the same harness on an open-weight model and the bill collapses while the score holds. GLM-5.3 running metareview at low effort (labelled “GLM vision · MRV · low” in the charts) scored 0.567 at $0.22 a review and found 72 bugs. Opus 5 running the same harness at the same effort (“Opus · MRV · low”) scored 0.567 at $2.97 and found 74. That is 1/13 the cost for the same score.

Why Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol? They were the strongest Anthropic and OpenAI models when running a harness, and each is priced a tier below its vendor’s flagship (price table → REPORT §3.4.1). GPT-5.6 Sol peaked at 0.535 (“Sol · CE · high”, $0.73 a review), below GLM vision · MRV · low on both score and price. Of the 30 closed-model harness configurations we completed, only Opus · CE · medium outscored GLM vision · MRV · low, and none was cheaper. Four of the top five scores overall are open-weight models.

Side by side: quality, price and time

Per pull-request review, across the same six pull requests.

The score is built from the bug, suggestion and unsupported columns. Verified bugs count most. A useful suggestion (an “accepted advisory” in the full report) is a finding that is true and worth a maintainer’s attention but is not a correctness bug or a style nit; it earns credit. An unsupported finding is a false claim, a style-only comment or vague speculation; it costs points. Bugs are proven by tests. Suggestions and unsupported findings are judged by a model, and a finding the judge was unsure about counts as neither. How the score works → REPORT §2.6

Check which ratio a vendor is quoting. Per token, GLM-5.3-Flash running metareview cost 1.7% of what Fable 5.1 cost as a one-shot prompt. Per review it cost 2.6%, because a harness review uses more tokens than a single prompt. Both are real; they are not the same number.

Harnesses at low effort had the best price/performance, and they finished fast. Within each model and harness, low effort was the fastest setting in 14 of 14 cases and the cheapest per verified bug in 10 of 14. GLM vision · MRV · low took 95 seconds a review, GLM flash · MRV · low took 79 seconds, and Opus · MRV · low took 100 seconds.

Turn the effort up and the open-weight models get slow. GLM-5.3 running metareview took about 38 minutes a review at medium effort and 49 minutes at high, against 3.1 minutes and 4.0 minutes for Opus 5 running the same harness. If a review has to come back while the author is still in context, stay at low effort.

Finding · effort

More thinking mostly bought a bigger bill

We compared high against medium reasoning effort for the same model and way of working, on the same pull requests. When the interval crosses zero, this sample cannot tell them apart. That happened in 17 of 22 comparisons, while high effort cost 0.96× to 4.2× as much.

How to read the bars. Each bar is a confidence interval: the range the true effect plausibly falls in, given how much the result moved from one pull request to the next. A high dot with a wide bar is not a win. Sonnet · MRV shows a gain of +0.09, but its interval runs from −0.07 to +0.26, so high effort may have helped, done nothing, or hurt. Each configuration ran once, so run-to-run variation is not in these bars; the real uncertainty is, if anything, wider.

The cost was reliable. The benefit was not. High effort cost more in 20 of 22 comparisons and found measurably more in four. The clearest gain was Opus · one-shot (+0.24, interval +0.08 to +0.39), and even there a harness was the better buy: Opus running either harness at low effort found more verified bugs (65 and 74) than Opus one-shot at high effort (47).

Effort did not add noise. High versus low was not formally tested, but on raw counts high effort found a mean of 5 more bugs out of 147 than low, and unsupported findings went down in 13 of 22 configurations and up in 4.

This needs more research. More pull requests and repeated runs could resolve effects this sample cannot. For now the effects are not large enough to justify recommending anything beyond this: use a harness, at low or medium effort. Open questions → REPORT §6

How we kept score

We only count bugs we could prove

The public benchmark’s human reviewers labelled 42 bugs in these pull requests. The models kept reporting more. So we audited them: every one of the 105 additional bugs has a test that fails on the pull request and passes on the fix. That gives 147 verified bugs to score against.

42bugs the benchmark labelled
147verified bugs after our audit
88the most any single configuration found
140found by all 66 configurations combined; seven were found by none

Why six pull requests, when the campaign ran 50? The full campaign made 2,416 healthy review runs over all 50 benchmark pull requests in five codebases. Proving every bug with a test is expensive, so we ran the complete grid, every model running every harness at every effort level, on the six highest-severity pull requests, picked by a criterion fixed in advance. That is the like-for-like set every comparison here uses.

Then we checked the slice against the rest, using the benchmark’s own labels across the 33 configurations that also ran all 50 pull requests. What held up: each configuration found about the same share of the labelled bugs on the six as on all 50 (a mean gap of +0.006), and the order of the models held or nearly held in four of five groupings.

What did not: harnesses looked better on the six than they do across the whole benchmark. On the other, lower-severity pull requests they raised more unsupported findings, which pulled their overall quality score (F1, which balances bugs caught against false alarms) down by about 0.08 on a zero-to-one scale. GLM-5.3 running metareview at low effort, for example, scored 0.73 on the six and 0.57 on all 50.

So read this page for which setup beats which, not for the exact scores, and expect a harness to be somewhat noisier on lower-severity pull requests than these numbers suggest. The full check → REPORT §2.4.2

What to do with this

On Monday

  1. Run a harness, not a single prompt.If your AI review is a single prompt over the diff, this is the biggest lever we measured: more verified bugs in 39 of 42 like-for-like comparisons.
  2. Pilot an open-weight model at low effort.Start with GLM-5.3 running metareview at low effort (“GLM vision · MRV · low” in the charts): $0.22 and about 95 seconds a review. GLM flash · MRV · low is the budget option at $0.024, with more unsupported findings to triage (19 against 5).
  3. Default to low or medium effort.Make high effort earn its place on your own pull requests before you pay for it everywhere.
  4. Measure triage time yourself.We measured bugs found, noise, dollars and seconds. We did not measure developer time saved, and that is the number that decides whether any of this is worth it for your team.

What this does not prove

  • The headline numbers come from six pull requests. They are the highest-severity ones in a 50-PR benchmark, from two of its five codebases. The check against the full benchmark covers recall and rankings on the benchmark’s own labels; the 147 verified bugs exist only for these six. This is evidence for running a pilot, not proof about your repository. §2.4
  • A single run per configuration. The intervals cover which pull requests were sampled, not run-to-run variation in the models. §4
  • Models did much of the judging. Verified bugs rest on executed tests, but “useful advice” and “unsupported” are model judgments, not human ones, and a GLM-family judge may favour GLM output. §2.6
  • Some runs have known gaps. The GLM harness runs predate a gateway fix, and Fable’s harness grid was not completed for budget, so Fable is excluded from the harness comparisons. §2.3.4
  • Costs are list prices (retrieved 2026-09-16), not invoices, and exclude failed gateway calls. §3.4.1
  • The author wrote one of the harnesses. See the disclosure at the top. §2.3.5
